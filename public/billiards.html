Billiards

Two equal spheres, a cloth and a rubber rail — and every one of the shelf's mechanics equations turned into a shot. Momentum and energy give the tangent line; friction between the balls gives throw; the tip's offset gives spin; the cloth turns that spin into follow, draw and stun. The lab below runs those equations live — set the shot up yourself: drag both balls, pick a pocket, and watch what speed does. It is the same physics, and the same numbers, that the Pool Sauce engine integrates.

What the lab leaves out

  • The cue is level. No swerve, no massé — side spin curves the cue ball only when the cue is elevated, and here it never is. Squirt (the cue ball leaving a shade off the cue's line with side) is a property of the cue, not the table, and is not modelled either: the speed and spin are taken as they leave the tip.
  • Balls stay on the cloth. No hop on a firm draw stroke, no jump; the cushion returns the ball in the plane.
  • Friction is Coulomb's — a constant coefficient, except ball–ball friction for throw, which uses Alciatore's measured fit μ = 0.01 + 0.108 e−1.088 v because slow shots really do throw more.
  • The cue ball's spin is untouched by the collision. The friction impulse between the balls also nudges the cue ball's spin (it is what makes the object ball pick up a little roll); that second-order effect is dropped, and the object ball leaves without spin.
  • Pockets are a mouth and a shelf, not jaws. Six 4½″ mouths on a 9-ft table, each with a shelf depth (1⅝″ corner, ⅜″ side — variables in the model): a ball whose centre crosses a mouth drops if it has that much roll left, and hangs in the jaws if not. No rattles or lip-outs — speed never rejects a ball the way a real corner does — and paths elsewhere stop where they meet a rail; rails are the cushion lab's business.
