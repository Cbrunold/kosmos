The catalogue so far

The Local Group

Everything gravitationally bound to us: two big spirals, a third smaller one, and forty-odd dwarfs strung out to the edge of the group's reach. Radius on the map is true distance on a log scale — each ring is ten times further out than it looks — and the angle groups objects by what they orbit, so it is not a direction on the sky.

Where this is soft

The distances and diameters here hold up — every apparent size they imply lands on the catalogued value, and Andromeda comes out at 3.4°, which is what you see. These are the parts that do not.

  1. The mass column mixes two different quantities. The Milky Way, Andromeda and the Large Magellanic Cloud are quoted at their halo masses — 1.2, 1.5 and 0.14 trillion suns, dark matter included. The Small Magellanic Cloud, M32, M110 and NGC 6822 are quoted at something closer to what shines. Both are legitimate numbers; printing them in one column is not, and it makes the LMC look twenty times the SMC when the ratio people usually mean is nearer two.
  2. Most objects carry no mass at all, and the empty ones are not marked as unknown so much as simply absent.
  3. The coordinates column is empty for every single object. With it, subgroup membership would be checkable in three dimensions — M32 and M110 should sit within a few hundred thousand light-years of Andromeda, and if they did not, something would be wrong. Without it, "Andromeda subgroup" is an assertion this page cannot test.
  4. Diameters for dwarf galaxies are a choice of definition, not a measurement: half-light radius, tidal radius and the visible extent differ by factors of two or three. Crater 2 is here at 1.06° and is often quoted at 2°, and both are honest — they are measuring different edges.
  5. Forty-five objects, of roughly eighty known members. The missing ones are mostly ultra-faint dwarfs found since 2015, and which of those are galaxies rather than star clusters is itself unsettled.

From the Cosmology databases in Notion. Observatory founding years and discovery years are stored as numbers rather than dates: ground sites are known to the year and space telescopes to the day, and one date column would have to either invent the former or discard the latter. Missions are the exception and keep real dates, because a launch is an instant somebody recorded — Apollo 11 left at 13:32 UTC on 16 July 1969. A mission with no end date is still running.
