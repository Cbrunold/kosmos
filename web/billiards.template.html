Billiards

Two equal spheres, a cloth and a rubber rail — and every one of the shelf's mechanics equations turned into a shot. Momentum and energy give the tangent line; friction between the balls gives throw; the tip's offset gives spin; the cloth turns that spin into follow, draw and stun. The lab below runs those equations live — set the shot up yourself: drag both balls, pick a pocket, and watch what speed does. It is the same physics, and the same numbers, that the Pool Sauce engine integrates.

Slide → roll

How quickly a struck ball becomes a rolling ball. Off the tip the ball slides; cloth friction eats the slip until surface speed matches ball speed — natural roll — and the slide distance grows with the square of the strike: double the strike, four times the slide. The amber line is your object ball at its current distance. Left of the crossing the cloth has taken the shot over before contact; right of it the cue ball arrives still sliding — which is where stun and draw live.

What the lab leaves out

  • The cue is almost level. Real strokes carry a few degrees of elevation, so side spin gets its real costs: squirt pushes the cue ball off the aim at the tip (≈2.5° at maximum side, a fixed mid-stiffness-shaft constant) and swerve curves it back while it slides — long, soft shots with side bend visibly, short firm ones barely. Full massé stays out of scope, and the collision now happens wherever the cue ball actually arrives, not where it was aimed.
  • Balls stay on the cloth. No hop on a firm draw stroke, no jump; the cushion returns the ball in the plane.
  • Friction is Coulomb's — a constant coefficient, except ball–ball friction for throw, which uses Alciatore's measured fit μ = 0.01 + 0.108 e−1.088 v because slow shots really do throw more.
  • Spin doesn't cross a collision. Every ball on the table is live — collisions between any two balls, both possibly moving, carry the full impulse-and-throw model — but the friction nudge each contact gives the striker's spin is dropped, and a struck ball leaves without spin of its own.
  • Pockets are a mouth and a shelf, not jaws. Six 4½″ mouths on a 9-ft table, each with a shelf depth (1⅝″ corner, ⅜″ side — variables in the model): a ball whose centre crosses a mouth drops if it has that much roll left, and hangs in the jaws if not. No rattles or lip-outs — speed never rejects a ball the way a real corner does. Rails rebound live in the main sim with the cushion lab's model — restitution √0.75 at a soft touch, deadening with pace to a floor of 0.55, plus friction and the ball's side transferring — so banks, kicks and multi-rail routes are real and hard shots die in a few rails instead of ricocheting forever; the jaw region bounces like plain rail.
