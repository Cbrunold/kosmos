Billiards

Two equal spheres, a cloth and a rubber rail — and every one of the shelf's mechanics equations turned into a shot. Momentum and energy give the tangent line; friction between the balls gives throw; the tip's offset gives spin; the cloth turns that spin into follow, draw and stun. The lab below runs those equations live. It is the same physics, and the same numbers, that the Pool Sauce engine integrates.

What the lab leaves out

  • The cue is level. No swerve, no massé — side spin curves the cue ball only when the cue is elevated, and here it never is. Squirt (the cue ball leaving a shade off the cue's line with side) is a property of the cue, not the table, and is not modelled either: the speed and spin are taken as they leave the tip.
  • Balls stay on the cloth. No hop on a firm draw stroke, no jump; the cushion returns the ball in the plane.
  • Friction is Coulomb's — a constant coefficient, except ball–ball friction for throw, which uses Alciatore's measured fit μ = 0.01 + 0.108 e−1.088 v because slow shots really do throw more.
  • The cue ball's spin is untouched by the collision. The friction impulse between the balls also nudges the cue ball's spin (it is what makes the object ball pick up a little roll); that second-order effect is dropped, and the object ball leaves without spin.
  • The frame is a 9-ft table without pockets — paths stop where they would meet a rail. Rails are the cushion lab's business.

Physics from the Equations shelf; constants from pool-sauce-engine. Coriolis, Théorie mathématique des effets du jeu de billard, 1835, had most of this; the throw model follows Alciatore (The Illustrated Principles of Pool and Billiards, technical proofs). Nothing here is a substitute for a table.
