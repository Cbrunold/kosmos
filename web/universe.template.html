The Universe in Three Dimensions

Everything catalogued here, placed by the direction it lies in and the distance to it, on a single radial axis that is logarithmic because nothing else could hold both the Oort Cloud and the horizon. Drag to turn it over. The rings are decades — each one is ten times further out than the last.

What this map does and does not know

The radial axis is honest and the angles are honest, but they are not honest in the same way, and the difference is drawn rather than hidden.

  1. A dot means we know which way it lies. The direction comes from the name resolver at Strasbourg — SIMBAD, then NED — not from anybody's memory, and every position on the page traces back to a catalogue entry.
  2. A ring means we know how far but not which way, so the map draws the whole sphere that the distance allows. The Sloan and CfA2 Great Walls are rings because they are tens of degrees across and have no single position to resolve. Structures we are inside are rings too, drawn at half their extent, which is where their edge actually is from here.
  3. The radius is the logarithm of distance, so shapes are wrong on purpose. A sphere is still a sphere and directions are exact, but anything with real extent is squashed toward us — the near side of a supercluster is pushed out and the far side pulled in. This is the only projection in which the Oort Cloud and the horizon appear in one picture, and the cost is that no volume here is the right shape.
  4. The planets are orbits, not places, and they go round the centre rather than round you. A planet's direction changes through the year and its distance from us changes with it, so a dot would state a position that is not a fact; what does not change is its distance from the Sun. Zoom in far enough and the label at the centre stops reading here and reads the Sun — this is the one point on the map where the origin and the observer come apart, and the Earth is the third ring out. Each orbit is drawn as a circle at its mean distance, in the plane of the ecliptic: eccentricity is not drawn (Mercury's is the only one you would notice — it really runs between 0.31 and 0.47 AU), and neither are the inclinations, which are 7.0° for Mercury, 3.4° for Venus and under 2.5° for the rest. The asteroid and Kuiper belts are bands, because a belt has no orbit and a single ring would claim one.
  5. Everything is drawn as it is now, not as it looks. Light from the far rings left before the Earth existed. A map of where things are and a map of what you see diverge completely past the Local Group, and this is the first.
  6. Distance is comoving, matching the ladder. Redshift is not converted to a light-travel time anywhere on this page.
  7. One object moves, and only radially. Run the clock and Andromeda closes; everything else holds still, because a measured velocity is the one thing the catalogue gives for it and does not give for the rest. M31 approaches the Milky Way at 110 km/s — that is the galactocentric figure, the heliocentric one folds in the Sun's own 220 km/s around the galactic centre and answers a different question. Drawing it as a straight radial fall is defensible only because Hubble measured its sideways motion too and found it small: 17 km/s, near enough head-on (van der Marel et al. 2012).
  8. The clock is not a prediction. It carries 110 km/s forward unchanged, which reaches contact in about 6.9 billion years. The accepted estimate is roughly 4 — the difference is gravity, which this page does not model. Two galaxies falling together accelerate, and the constant-velocity arithmetic is wrong by that acceleration and in that direction. Believe the shrinking distance, not the arrival time.
